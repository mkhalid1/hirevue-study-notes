# Situational questions

**In one line:** you are given a work scenario and asked what you would do, or asked to respond as if the situation were happening now, and you answer with clear steps and the reason for each.

HireVue's candidate FAQ describes two related types. **Situational judgement questions** ask you to explain what you would do in a hypothetical scenario. **Scenario-based simulations** ask you to act out your response, for example speaking to an upset customer as if they were in front of you. This guide covers both, because the thinking underneath is the same.

## What it measures

A situational question tests judgement: can you read a situation, see what matters most, and choose sensible actions in a sensible order? The employer chooses which competencies matter for the job. For a customer role it may be care and problem-solving. For a team role, communication and fairness. For a sales role, HireVue's own example is negotiation.

Good answers tend to share four things:

- they **deal with the urgent part first**, especially anything about safety or a customer waiting
- they **gather facts** before blaming anyone
- they **follow sensible rules** and involve the right person when something is outside your authority
- they **close the loop**: check it worked and tell the people who need to know

## How the questions look

In our own words, typical prompts look like this:

- "A customer tells you their order arrived damaged and they need it tomorrow. What would you do?"
- "You notice a teammate keeps missing their part of a shared task. How would you handle it?"
- "Imagine I am a customer who has been waiting 40 minutes. Respond to me now."

For simulations, the on-screen text or video usually sets you up as a person in the scene. You then speak to the camera as that person, not about them.

## A method that works

Use four steps. We call them read, rank, respond, follow up. This is our structure, not HireVue's.

1. **Read.** Say back the key problem in one sentence, so you show you understood it: "So the customer needs the item tomorrow and it arrived broken."
2. **Rank.** Decide what comes first. Safety and a person in distress come before paperwork. A deadline that someone else depends on comes before a nice-to-have.
3. **Respond.** Give your actions in order, and say *why* for each one. "I'd apologise first, because they're stressed and they need to know someone is on it."
4. **Follow up.** Say how you would check it worked, and who you would update.

If you have done something similar before, you can add one sentence about it: "I had something like this at the café, and offering two options worked well." Keep it short. The question asked what you *would* do.

For **simulations**, switch to speaking directly: "I'm really sorry about this. Let me sort out a replacement for you now." Keep a warm, steady tone and still follow the same four steps, just in the first person to the "customer" or "colleague".

## Traps that cost points

- **"I'd follow company policy" and nothing else.** That tells the listener nothing about your judgement. Say what you would actually do.
- **Jumping to escalation.** Passing everything to a manager can look like avoiding the problem. Escalate when it is outside your authority or after your first step has not worked.
- **Confronting people in public.** Raising a colleague's behaviour in front of others or a customer rarely helps.
- **Ignoring the urgent part.** A spill in a busy aisle, a customer about to leave, a deadline in an hour: deal with that first.
- **Inventing big facts.** If the scenario does not say something important, make one reasonable assumption out loud and carry on.
- **Narrating during a simulation.** "I would tell her that I'm sorry" is weaker than saying "I'm sorry" to her.
- **No follow-up.** Ending at the action leaves out the check that it worked.

## Timing

Your thinking and answer times are set by the employer and shown before you start. As our guidance: use the first few seconds of thinking time to decide the single most important action, then build around it. In a 2-minute answer, spend about 10 seconds on "read", 15 on "rank", 70 on "respond" and 20 on "follow up".

## Practice items

These 3 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from HireVue or any employer. Each has one best option. Choose before opening the explanations.

**Item 1.** "A customer tells you their order arrived damaged and they need it tomorrow. What would you do?" Which outline is best?

- A. "I'd follow company policy for damaged items."
- B. "I'd tell them to call head office, since they handle complaints."
- C. "I'd apologise and check the order details and photos of the damage. Then I'd offer the fastest fix we have, such as an express replacement or collection from a nearby branch, and confirm the timing with them. I'd note the damage for the supplier and check the next day that it arrived."

**Item 2.** "Your manager needs a report from you by 3 p.m. At 2 p.m. a senior colleague asks for an hour of help with something else." Which outline is best?

- A. Tell the colleague about your 3 p.m. deadline, offer to help straight after, and if they say it cannot wait, check with your manager which should come first.
- B. Drop the report and help the colleague, because they are senior.
- C. Ignore the request until the report is done.

**Item 3.** A simulation says: "I am a customer whose delivery is three days late. Speak to me now." Which opening is best?

- A. "I would tell the customer that I understand and that I'll look into it."
- B. "I'm sorry your delivery is late, that's frustrating. Can I take your order number so I can see exactly where it is?"
- C. "Deliveries are handled by a courier, so it's not really something we control."

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: C.** It apologises, gathers facts, offers a fast fix, confirms timing and follows up. *Why not A:* naming policy shows no judgement. *Why not B:* passing the customer away when you could help leaves them stuck.

**Item 2: A.** You protect the deadline, offer real help, and let the manager decide if the two clash. *Why not B:* seniority alone does not cancel a commitment to your manager. *Why not C:* ignoring a colleague is unhelpful and could hide a real emergency.

**Item 3: B.** It speaks to the customer directly, acknowledges how they feel and moves to fact-finding. *Why not A:* it narrates instead of acting out the response, which the simulation asked for. *Why not C:* it shifts blame and helps nobody.

</details>

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: a hypothetical question

"A new team member seems to be struggling but has not asked for help. What would you do?"

| Step | What you might say (about 270 words spoken) |
|---|---|
| Read | "So someone new is finding it hard, and they might not feel able to ask." |
| Rank | "The first thing is to check in without making it a big deal, because new people often worry about looking slow." |
| Respond | "I'd ask them for a coffee or a quick chat and ask how they're finding things. If something specific is tricky, I'd show them how I do it or point them to the right guide. I'd let them know they can message me with small questions. If it looks like a bigger issue, like their workload, I'd encourage them to talk to our manager, and offer to go with them." |
| Follow up | "I'd check in again at the end of the week. When I started at the library, someone did that for me and it made a real difference." |

### Worked example 2: a simulation

"I'm a customer. I was charged twice for the same order. Talk to me."

| Step | What you might say directly to the camera |
|---|---|
| Read | "I'm sorry, being charged twice is stressful. Let me check that for you." |
| Rank | "Can I take your order number and the last four digits of the card? I'll look at both charges now." |
| Respond | "I can see the two payments. I'm going to refund the duplicate now. It usually shows on your statement within a few days, and I'll send you an email confirming it." |
| Follow up | "Is there anything else on the order I can help with? I'll also flag this so we can find out why it happened." |

Notice that every line is spoken *to* the customer. That is the main difference from a hypothetical answer.

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | C | A | B |

- **3:** your judgement instincts are good. Practise saying answers out loud with the four steps.
- **2:** check whether your misses were about order (urgent first) or channel (private first, escalate later).
- **1 or fewer:** reread "Traps that cost points", then redo the items in two days.
