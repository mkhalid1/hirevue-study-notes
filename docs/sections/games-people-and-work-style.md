# People and work-style games

**In one line:** game-like tasks about how you prefer to work and how you read and respond to other people, from picking which picture is more like you to handling a short customer or team scenario.

HireVue's game-based assessments cover three domains, and two of them live here: **Personality and Work Style** (how you approach tasks and responsibilities) and **Working with People** (collaboration, communication and interpersonal skills). HireVue's own articles give examples: a work-style game that shows two photos and asks which is more like you, work simulations such as resolving a customer complaint, and tasks about recognising emotions and showing empathy. HireVue does not publish game names or scoring rules for these.

This page is about understanding these tasks so you can answer them honestly and calmly. It does not try to tell you which preferences to claim. Work-style items describe you, and the most useful thing you can do is describe yourself accurately.

## What it measures

**Work-style tasks** build a picture of your preferences: busy or quiet surroundings, planning ahead or adapting as you go, working alone or with people. HireVue says its psychologists match the games to the competencies a role needs. There is no single "best" profile across all jobs: a preference that suits one role may suit another less, which is exactly why answering as yourself matters.

**People tasks** look at skills that can be seen in behaviour:

- **reading emotions:** noticing what someone is likely feeling from what they say and do
- **empathy in action:** responding in a way that acknowledges that feeling
- **practical judgement:** choosing a response that helps solve the problem

Unlike work-style items, people scenarios usually do have better and worse responses, in the same way a situational question does.

## How the questions look

In our own words:

- two pictures or short descriptions side by side, and you pick the one closer to you
- a short scene, a message or a face, and you choose what the person is most likely feeling
- a customer or colleague situation that unfolds over a few steps, where you pick a reply at each step
- a chat-style exchange where you respond to incoming messages

## A method that works

For **work-style** choices:

1. **Answer for your usual self at work,** not your best day or your worst.
2. **Pick the closer option** when neither fits perfectly. These tasks expect that.
3. **Go with your first sensible reading.** Long deliberation tends to make answers less accurate, not more.
4. **Stay consistent because you are being honest,** not because you are tracking what you said earlier.

For **people** scenarios:

1. **Name the feeling.** What is this person most likely feeling, given what they said and did?
2. **Acknowledge it before solving.** People listen better once they feel heard.
3. **Move to a practical next step.** Empathy without action leaves the problem unsolved.
4. **Keep it respectful and within your role.** No blame, no promises you cannot keep.

## Traps that cost points

- **Trying to guess the "ideal" work-style answer.** You do not know what the role's profile looks like, and a profile that is not you is a poor basis for a job you then have to do.
- **Freezing on two imperfect options.** Choose the nearer one and move on.
- **Reading only the words in an emotion task.** Context matters: a short reply after a long wait reads differently from the same reply in a relaxed chat.
- **Solving before acknowledging.** In people scenarios, jumping straight to policy can come across as cold.
- **Acknowledging without solving.** Saying "I understand" five times is not a response.
- **Blaming someone else** in a customer or team scene.

## Timing

HireVue says each game is under 20 minutes. Work-style choices are usually quick, often a few seconds each. As our guidance, if you are taking more than about 10 seconds on a "which is more like you" choice, take the closer option and move on.

## Practice items

These 3 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from HireVue or any game. Item 1 checks your understanding of work-style tasks. Item 2 practises reading emotions. Item 3 practises people scenarios. Each has one best option.

**Item 1.** In a work-style task you see two pictures: a tidy desk and a busy desk covered in notes. Which statement is accurate?

- A. One picture is the correct answer, and the game is checking whether you can spot it.
- B. You should always pick the tidy desk.
- C. There is no right answer. The task records your preference, so pick the one closer to how you really work.

**Item 2.** A customer writes: "I've now explained this three times to three different people." What are they most likely feeling?

- A. Frustrated
- B. Grateful
- C. Confused about the product

**Item 3.** A customer in a chat-style task is angry that a refund has not arrived. You can see it was sent two days ago and can take up to five working days. Which reply is best?

- A. "It's been sent, so there's nothing I can do."
- B. "I can see how worrying it is not to see the money yet. The refund was sent two days ago, and it can take up to five working days to show. If it hasn't arrived by then, reply here and I'll chase it with the bank for you."
- C. "I'll make sure it arrives tomorrow."

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: C.** Work-style choices describe your preferences. HireVue matches the overall picture to the role, and you cannot know the role's profile from one item. *Why not A and B:* treating a preference item as a puzzle with one answer leads to a profile that is not you.

**Item 2: A.** Repeating a problem to several people is a classic cause of frustration. *Why not B:* nothing suggests thanks. *Why not C:* the complaint is about being passed around, not about not understanding the product.

**Item 3: B.** It acknowledges the worry, gives accurate facts and offers a real next step. *Why not A:* accurate but cold, and it leaves the customer with nothing. *Why not C:* it promises something you cannot control.

</details>

## Worked examples

### Worked example 1: a work-style choice

Two descriptions appear: "I like to plan my week in detail on Monday" and "I like to decide what to do each day as things come up."

What it is about: how you organise your time. Neither is better in general. A role full of fixed deadlines may suit planners, and a role full of incoming requests may suit people who adapt. You cannot tell from one item which the employer values, and HireVue says the mix of games is set per role.

A sensible approach: think of the last two weeks at work or study. Which did you actually do more often? Pick that one.

### Worked example 2: a customer simulation over three steps

**Step 1.** A customer says their new headphones stopped working after a week. *A good choice acknowledges and asks:* "I'm sorry, that's annoying after a week. Can you tell me what happens when you turn them on?"

**Step 2.** They say there is no sound from the left side. *A good choice checks the simple fix first:* "Could you try them with a different device? That tells us whether it's the headphones or the connection."

**Step 3.** It is still silent on the left. *A good choice offers the real remedy and the timing:* "That sounds like a fault, so I'll arrange a replacement. You'll get a returns label by email today."

Each step does one job: acknowledge, diagnose, resolve. That pattern is what these scenarios tend to reward, and it is also simply good service.

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | C | A | B |

- **3:** you read people well and respond sensibly. In the real games, keep your work-style answers honest and quick.
- **2:** look at whether misses were in reading feelings (item 2) or responding (item 3). Most response misses come from skipping either the acknowledgement or the action.
- **1 or fewer:** reread the four people-scenario steps and practise them on everyday situations, such as a friend's complaint or a support chat you have had.
