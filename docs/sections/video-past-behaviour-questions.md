# Past-behaviour questions

**In one line:** you are asked to describe a real time you did something, and you answer with one specific story told in a clear order.

These are the "Tell us about a time when..." questions. HireVue's candidate FAQ lists them as one of the three main question types, and its interview tips suggest writing brief stories about challenges you've faced, the approach you took and the results, then keeping notes nearby. That advice is the whole of this page, made practical.

## What it measures

A past-behaviour question asks for evidence. The idea behind it is simple: how you handled something before is a fair guide to how you will handle something similar again. HireVue's examples of what an assessment can look for include teamwork, persistence and, for a sales job, negotiation. The employer picks the competencies for the role, so the same question can be checking different things in different jobs.

What the question rewards, whatever the competency:

- a **real, specific** situation, not a general claim about yourself
- **your** part in it, not the team's
- **what you actually did**, step by step
- **a result** you can name, plus what you learned

## How the questions look

You see the question on screen as text or a short video of someone asking it. Some typical shapes, in our own words:

- "Tell us about a time you had to deal with a difficult customer."
- "Describe a situation where you had to work with someone whose style was very different from yours."
- "Give an example of a goal you set and how you reached it."
- "Talk about a time something went wrong and what you did about it."

You then get some thinking time and a fixed time to answer. Both are set by the employer and shown on screen before you start, so read them in the setup screens rather than relying on anything you have read online.

## A method that works

STAR is the standard structure, and it works because it matches how a listener follows a story.

| Part | What you say | Rough share of a 2-minute answer |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Where you were and what was going on, in one or two sentences | about 15 to 20 seconds |
| **T**ask | What you were responsible for, or what the problem was | about 10 seconds |
| **A**ction | What *you* did, in order, with the reason for each step | about 60 to 75 seconds |
| **R**esult | What happened, with a number or a concrete outcome if you have one, and what you learned | about 20 to 30 seconds |

These timings are our guidance, not HireVue's. Most people speak at roughly 130 to 150 words a minute when they are calm, so a 2-minute answer is about 260 to 300 words and a 3-minute answer is about 400 to 450.

Step by step, when the question appears:

1. **Name the competency.** Read the question once and ask what it is really about: conflict, pressure, customer care, learning, leadership.
2. **Pick the story.** Choose from your prepared list the example that fits most directly. One good story beats two half stories.
3. **Jot four words.** One word each for S, T, A and R on your notepad. That is enough to keep you on track.
4. **Start with the situation, fast.** "In my second year working at a garden centre, we had a stock system failure the week before a bank holiday."
5. **Spend most of the time on action.** Use "I". Say what you did first, then next, and why.
6. **Land the result.** A number, a decision someone made, a change that stuck. Then one sentence on what you took from it.
7. **Stop.** You do not have to fill every second. A clean ending is better than a trailing one.

Before the interview, build a **story bank** of six to eight stories you can adapt: a time you solved a problem, handled conflict, worked under pressure, made a mistake, helped a customer or colleague, learned something fast, led or influenced others, and changed your mind. Most past-behaviour questions can be answered from that set.

## Traps that cost points

- **Answering in general.** "I'm always calm under pressure" is a claim. The question asked for a time. Give one.
- **Long set-up.** If the situation takes a full minute, the action part gets squeezed. Cut background to what the listener needs.
- **"We" all the way through.** Teams matter, but the question is about you. Say what you did inside the team.
- **No result.** Stories that stop at the action leave the listener guessing whether it worked.
- **The wrong story.** A strong story that answers a different question still misses. Match the story to the competency.
- **Skipping half of a two-part question.** "What did you do, and what would you do differently?" needs both parts.
- **Inventing or polishing past the truth.** You may be asked about the same story in a later live interview. Use real events.

## Timing

Read the answer time in your setup screen and plan to finish with 10 to 20 seconds to spare. If your thinking time is short, do not try to script sentences: pick the story and jot S, T, A, R. If you lose your place mid-answer, say the next action you took. Picking the story back up at the action step is easier than restarting.

## Practice items

These 3 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from HireVue or any employer. Each one has one best option. Commit to an answer before you open the explanations.

**Item 1.** The question is: "Tell us about a time you had to meet a tight deadline." Which answer outline fits best?

- A. "I work really well under pressure. I like deadlines because they keep me focused, and my managers have always said I'm reliable."
- B. "Last spring at the print shop, a customer needed 300 event programmes by 9 a.m. the next day after their designer dropped out. I was the only one on shift who knew the layout software, so I rebuilt the file from their notes, asked my manager to approve overtime for one printer operator, and ran a proof at 6 p.m. so the customer could sign it off. We delivered at 8:30. The customer has used us for every event since."
- C. "Our team once had a deadline for a big project. We all worked late and in the end we got it done, which was a relief for everyone."

**Item 2.** This outline is missing one STAR part. Which one? "At the café where I worked, two new staff kept getting orders wrong on busy Saturdays. I asked the manager if I could run a ten-minute walkthrough before each Saturday shift. I wrote a one-page cheat sheet of the most common orders and paired each new person with an experienced one for the first hour."

- A. Situation
- B. Task
- C. Action
- D. Result

**Item 3.** The question is: "Tell us about a mistake you made at work." Which outline is strongest?

- A. "My biggest mistake is that I care too much and take on too much work."
- B. "I sent a customer the wrong quote because the pricing sheet I'd been given was out of date. That was really the system's fault."
- C. "I booked a delivery for the wrong date because I misread a handwritten note. I spotted it the next morning, called the customer before the van left, rebooked it, and apologised. Since then I repeat dates back to customers in writing before I confirm."

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: B.** It names a real situation, your responsibility, the steps you took and a result with a time and a follow-on outcome. *Why not A:* it is a list of claims with no example, which is the most common way to miss a past-behaviour question. *Why not C:* it is vague and all "we", so the listener cannot tell what you did.

**Item 2: D, Result.** The situation (orders going wrong on Saturdays), the task (fixing it, implied by asking the manager) and the actions (walkthrough, cheat sheet, pairing) are all there. Nothing says whether the errors fell. *Why not B:* the task is light but clear from context. A missing result is the bigger gap.

**Item 3: C.** A real mistake, owned, fixed quickly, with a habit changed afterwards. *Why not A:* a strength dressed as a weakness does not answer the question. *Why not B:* it shifts the blame and has no fix or lesson.

</details>

## Worked examples

These show the full shape of a strong answer. Read them for structure, then build your own from your own experience. Copying them word for word would not help: an employer's later interviews will ask about your real stories.

### Worked example 1: "Tell us about a time you dealt with an unhappy customer."

| Part | Notes (about 280 words spoken, roughly 2 minutes) |
|---|---|
| Situation | Saturday at a phone shop. A customer came back angry because his new phone would not transfer contacts, and he had a flight that evening. |
| Task | I was the one on the floor. I needed to calm things down and get his phone working before he left. |
| Action | Let him finish without interrupting. Repeated back the problem so he knew I had it right. Told him what I would try and how long it would take. Found that his old phone's backup was switched off, turned it on, ran the backup, and moved the contacts by cable. Checked three contacts with him before he left. |
| Result | Done in 25 minutes. He left a positive review naming me. I added a backup check to the setup checklist I used with every new phone. |

### Worked example 2: "Describe a time you had to manage several priorities at once."

| Part | Notes (about 400 words spoken, roughly 3 minutes) |
|---|---|
| Situation | Final term at university, part-time job in a library, and treasurer of the hiking society during its annual trip planning. |
| Task | Hand in a dissertation, cover my library shifts and get the trip budget signed off, all in the same fortnight. |
| Action | Listed every deadline on one page. Asked the library for a shift swap two weeks ahead and covered it later. Split the dissertation into daily targets of 600 words. Delegated the trip bookings to the secretary and kept only the budget. Checked progress each Sunday evening and adjusted. |
| Result | Dissertation in two days early, no missed shifts, budget approved on time. I still plan any busy patch on one page and review it weekly. |

### Worked example 3: build your own

Pick the story from your bank that fits "a time you changed someone's mind". Write one line for S, one for T, three to five short lines for A and one or two for R. Record yourself answering it out loud, then watch it back and check: did you say "I", did you give a result, did you finish in time?

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | B | D | C |

- **3:** you can spot a strong answer. Now record yourself giving three of your own, because spotting and doing are different skills.
- **2:** look at which trap caught you. Most misses here are about vague results or "we" stories.
- **1 or fewer:** reread the STAR table and the method, then redo the items tomorrow before building your story bank.
