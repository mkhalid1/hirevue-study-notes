# Numerical games

**In one line:** short, fast tasks where you work with numbers: quick sums, percentages, number patterns and picking the right calculation, usually against a clock.

HireVue does not publish the names or exact rules of its games. What it does say is that its game-based assessments cover a domain called **Working with Information** (problem-solving, critical thinking and decision-making), that games are randomized, and that you move up or down in levels depending on how you do. HireVue's articles on game-based assessment describe numerical tasks as interpreting numbers, spotting patterns and solving maths problems quickly. This guide trains those skills so that whatever the game looks like on screen, the thinking is familiar.

## What it measures

Numerical games test number fluency under time pressure rather than advanced maths. In practice that means:

- **mental arithmetic**: adding, multiplying and dividing small numbers quickly and accurately
- **proportions**: percentages, fractions and ratios
- **patterns**: seeing the rule in a sequence of numbers
- **choosing the operation**: knowing which calculation answers the question

None of this needs more than school maths. Speed and accuracy come from practice.

## How the questions look

Because games vary by employer, expect the look to change. In our own words, common shapes are:

- a target number and a few tiles or values that you combine to reach it
- a sequence with a missing value
- two quantities where you pick the larger or the better deal
- a short scenario ("a delivery van travels...") with one number to work out

Many game formats get harder as you get answers right. If a round suddenly feels tough, that can simply mean the game has moved you up a level.

## A method that works

1. **Read the question once, carefully.** Underline in your head what is being asked: a total, a difference, a percentage, the next number.
2. **Estimate first.** Round the numbers and get a rough answer. 49 × 21 is about 50 × 20 = 1,000. If the exact answer you calculate is far from the estimate, check it.
3. **Pick the fastest route.** 25% is a quarter. 10% is moving the decimal point. Multiplying by 5 is multiplying by 10 and halving.
4. **For sequences, look at the gaps.** Write the differences between neighbours. If those are not steady, try ratios (each term doubled, tripled, halved) or "double then add one".
5. **Check units and direction.** A rise from 80 to 100 is 25%. A fall from 100 to 80 is 20%. The starting value is the base.
6. **Commit and move on.** A long pause on one item usually costs more than an occasional wrong answer.

## Traps that cost points

- **Using the wrong base for a percentage.** Percentage change is always the change divided by the starting value.
- **Doing exact sums when an estimate would decide.** If the options are 900, 1,000 and 1,100, you only need the estimate.
- **Assuming every sequence adds a fixed amount.** Many multiply, or have gaps that grow.
- **Forgetting the order of operations.** Multiplication and division come before addition and subtraction unless brackets say otherwise.
- **Freezing after a wrong answer.** HireVue's own advice is to keep going after a failed round. Later rounds may be easier.
- **Using a calculator or someone else's help** when the game rules do not allow it. It is unfair to other candidates and does not reflect what you can do.

## Timing

HireVue says each game takes under 20 minutes. Within a game, items are usually quick. As our guidance, aim for about 15 to 30 seconds per simple item in practice. If you cannot see a route within 20 seconds, estimate, choose and continue, if the game lets you.

## Practice items

These 3 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from HireVue or any game. Use no calculator. Time yourself: aim for under 1 minute for all 3.

**Item 1.** What comes next? 3, 7, 15, 31, ?

**Item 2.** Weekly orders rise from 80 to 100. What is the percentage increase?

**Item 3.** Which is closest to 49 × 21?

- A. 900
- B. 1,000
- C. 1,100

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 3</summary>

**Item 1: 63.** Each term is double the previous one plus 1: 3 × 2 + 1 = 7, 7 × 2 + 1 = 15, 15 × 2 + 1 = 31, 31 × 2 + 1 = 63. The gaps (4, 8, 16) also double, so the next gap is 32.

**Item 2: 25%.** The rise is 20, and the starting value is 80. 20 ÷ 80 = 0.25.

**Item 3: B.** 49 × 21 is about 50 × 20 = 1,000. Exactly, it is 1,029, which is still closest to 1,000.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | 63 | 25% | B |

- **3, under 1 minute:** your number fluency is solid. Practise speed with mixed sets.
- **2:** check whether misses were percentages (base errors) or sequences (wrong rule type). Drill that type.
- **1 or fewer, or over 2 minutes:** spend 10 minutes a day on mental arithmetic for a week: times tables to 12, percentages of round numbers, and simple sequences.
